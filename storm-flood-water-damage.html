Which parts of San Bruno flood during winter storms?

Low areas that drain to San Bruno Creek, especially Belle Air near SFO, see the most flooding. Downhill homes in hillside neighborhoods get runoff in garages and crawl spaces.

San Bruno's flooding comes from two directions. Near the Bay, stormwater from much of the city flows to San Bruno Creek, which empties into the Bay close to SFO. When heavy rain lines up with a high tide, the creek cannot drain, and water backs up into streets and homes around 7th Avenue and SR-380. Up in the hills, Crestmoor, Rollingwood, Portola Highlands and Pacific Heights send runoff downhill fast, flooding garages, in-law units and crawl spaces on the low side of the street.

Common Types of Storm Water Damage in San Bruno, CA

Storm water damage in San Bruno, CA usually shows up in one of these ways:

  • Water coming under exterior doors and garage doors
  • Runoff seeping through foundation walls into crawl spaces
  • Floor drains and toilets backing up when the sewer system is overloaded
  • Roof and window leaks from wind-driven rain
  • Flooded garages, converted garages and in-law units at ground level
  • Street water entering low homes and businesses during king tides and heavy rain

Why Floodwater Is Treated Differently

Water that comes from outside is classified as Category 3, or black water. It can carry sewage, chemicals, oil from roads, animal waste and bacteria. That means flood cleanup is not just extraction and drying:

  • Carpet, pad and upholstered items that touched floodwater are removed
  • Drywall and insulation are cut out above the water line
  • All surfaces are cleaned and disinfected
  • Workers wear full protective gear
  • Drying only starts once contaminated materials are out

Atmospheric River Flooding and the Peninsula

Most of San Bruno's flooding happens between November and March, during atmospheric river flooding events that bring days of heavy rain off the Pacific. Storms like these saturate the hillsides, overwhelm storm drains, and raise creek levels at the same time. When several storms arrive back to back, the ground cannot absorb any more water, and homes that stayed dry in earlier storms suddenly take on water.

Flood Insurance and Storm Claims

Standard homeowners policies usually do not cover flooding from outside water such as rising creeks or street water. That generally needs a separate flood policy. Wind-driven rain through a damaged roof, or a sewer backup with the right endorsement, may be covered under a homeowners policy. Every policy is different. The crew documents all damage with photos and moisture readings so you have what you need for any claim.

Flood Damage FAQs

How soon should flood cleanup start?

+

As soon as the water stops rising and it is safe to enter. Mold can start within 24 to 72 hours, and contaminated water spreads bacteria the longer it sits.

Can furniture be saved after a flood?

+

Hard furniture like solid wood or metal can often be cleaned and disinfected. Upholstered furniture and mattresses that touched floodwater usually cannot be saved.

Do I need a separate flood policy?

+

Outside floodwater is usually excluded from standard homeowners policies. If you live in a low area near San Bruno Creek, ask your agent about flood coverage.

Is it safe to stay in the house during cleanup?

+

If only a garage or one area flooded and the power is safe, many families stay home. If living areas took floodwater, it is usually better to stay elsewhere until the area is clean and dry.

What if my sewer backed up during the storm?

+

That is sewage and needs Category 3 cleanup. See sewage backup cleanup.

Storm Damage vs Flood Damage: Why the Difference Matters

The words sound alike, but insurance companies and restoration crews treat them differently. Storm damage usually means water that got in because something broke: wind lifted shingles, a tree limb cracked the roof, or rain was driven through a damaged window. Flood damage usually means water that rose from outside, such as a creek overflowing, street water entering the garage, or groundwater coming up through the floor. Storm damage is often covered by a homeowners policy, while flood damage usually needs a separate flood policy. The cleanup also differs: rainwater through a roof may be Category 1 or 2, while rising floodwater is treated as Category 3.

What the First 24 Hours After a Flood Look Like

  • Hours 0 to 2: water stops rising; stay out until it is safe and power is off in wet areas
  • Hours 2 to 6: the crew arrives, pumps out standing water and removes mud
  • Hours 6 to 12: contaminated carpet, pad and soaked drywall come out; surfaces are cleaned and disinfected
  • Hours 12 to 24: dehumidifiers and air movers are set up and first moisture readings are logged

Getting through these steps in the first day is what keeps a flood from turning into a mold problem.

Protecting Important Papers and Valuables

In low areas that flood, keep documents, photos and valuables safe before storm season:

  • Store birth certificates, passports, deeds and insurance policies in a waterproof container on an upper shelf
  • Scan important papers and photos and keep digital copies
  • Keep a home inventory with photos or video of rooms and belongings
  • Move heirlooms and electronics out of garages and ground floors

After a flood, those records make insurance claims much easier and protect what cannot be replaced.

After the Storm: Checking for Hidden Damage

Even if water did not come inside, a big storm can leave hidden problems. Walk around the house and look for missing shingles, clogged gutters, soil washed away from the foundation and water marks on garage walls. Check the attic for damp insulation and the crawl space for standing water. Catching these early prevents slow leaks and mold from building up before the next storm arrives.
